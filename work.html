How Results Are Reported Here

Four rules, applied to every case study on this site. They're unusual enough to be worth stating.

The starting point is always shown. A percentage without a baseline is not evidence. Going from 9 clicks to 81 is +800% and it is also 81 clicks — both facts belong in the same sentence.

The timeframe is always shown. Fourteen weeks and fourteen months are different claims, and anyone omitting the period is hoping you'll assume the shorter one.

What got worse is included. Two of these four saw average position decline. Two saw click-through rate fall. Both are the predictable trade from expanding query coverage — and a case study that reports only the good half is not a case study.

What isn't known is stated. Where no revenue data was supplied, that's written down rather than implied around.

Common Questions

Why are the client names removed?

At client request. Where a case study names a business, it's because that business agreed to it. References matched to your situation are available on request — a SaaS founder wants to speak to a SaaS founder, not to a local services client.

Why is every case study reporting things that went wrong?

Because they did, and because a case study with no failures in it has been edited into fiction. Average position worsened in two of these four. CTR fell in two. Those are real trade-offs from expanding query coverage, and hiding them would make the rest less believable, not more.

These numbers look modest compared to other agencies.

They're baselines and timeframes reported honestly. A jump from 200 to 1,000 sessions is 400% growth and also almost nothing. Every figure here states where it started and over what period, which is what makes it checkable.

Can you share revenue figures?

Only where the client supplied them, and in two of these four they didn't. Clicks are not bookings and traffic is not revenue — case studies that blur those two are the reason this industry has a credibility problem.
